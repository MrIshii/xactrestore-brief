Senior UX design for complex products and workflows

Turning Complexity Into Simplicity

My recent work at Verisk involved enterprise software connecting complex workflows, people, information, and business processes.

Because the products are proprietary, I don't reproduce Verisk screens or internal artifacts here.

Instead, this brief shows how I approach complex product problems, what research taught us, and how those insights became design decisions.

I Enjoy Making Complex Workflows Feel Simple

The more complicated the system becomes, the more interesting the UX problem becomes.

Give me the complicated problem.

I enjoy the work that happens before the answer is obvious: understanding the system, finding the real problem, bringing the right people together, and turning complexity into something users don't have to think about.

My Design Process

Design Thinking is the foundation. Senior UX design adds another level of awareness.

I follow the principles of Design Thinking: understand people, define the right problem, explore possibilities, prototype, test, and iterate.

As a Senior UX Designer, I also have to maintain the 10,000-foot view throughout that process.

It's a loop, not a handoff.

Recognize→Empathize + Discover→Ideate + Align→Simplify→Prototype→Test→Ship→Learn ↻

  1. RECOGNIZE

    Recognize the problem, along with the product, business goals, technology, constraints, and surrounding system.

  2. EMPATHIZE + DISCOVER

    We do interviews to learn how users think, learn about their workflows, and how the problem affects them.

  3. IDEATE + ALIGN

    Explore multiple ways to solve the right problem, bringing user evidence together with Product, Engineering, technology, effort, and business priorities.

  4. SIMPLIFY

    Remove unnecessary complexity and organize the experience around what the user is trying to accomplish.

  5. PROTOTYPE

    Turn the idea into something real enough to test.

  6. TEST

    Put ideas in front of users while they're still inexpensive to change.

  7. SHIP

    Deliver an intentional, coherent experience, not something that feels unfinished simply because the product will continue evolving.

  8. LEARN

    Use real-world behavior, feedback, and business results to inform what comes next.

And throughout that loop, I keep four perspectives in view

User↔Product↔Engineering↔Business

Three Principles Guide My Decisions

The process tells me how to work. These principles help me decide.

01
See the whole product

Advocate for the user. Understand the business. Design for both.

A feature doesn't exist by itself.

I consider user needs, product strategy, technical feasibility, engineering effort, business goals, budget, schedule, and what today's decision may affect tomorrow.

Senior design requires seeing beyond the screen being designed.

02
Make complexity feel simple

The system can be complicated. The experience shouldn't be.

  • What does the user need now?
  • What can wait?
  • What can the system anticipate?
  • What can we eliminate?
  • Where will people hesitate or make mistakes?

I organize the experience around the user's goals and mental model, not the complexity of the system underneath it.

Keep the capability. Remove the burden.

03
Advocate strongly. Hold the solution loosely.

Research gives us evidence. Collaboration gives us better answers.

UX should have a strong point of view about the user.

That doesn't mean UX owns every answer.

I want Product, Engineering, and Design to collaborate and resolve the problem, encouraging open communication, challenging assumptions, sharing expertise, and shaping solutions.

  • Challenge assumptions.
  • Surface technical opportunities.
  • Explore alternatives.
  • Solve the problem together.

My responsibility is to advocate for the user, not protect my first design.

When the team helps discover the solution, they don't simply receive it. They own it, and that shared ownership produces a better product.

Putting It Into Practice

XactRestore Lead Intake

The Team

Product ManagerLead EngineerEngineerSenior UX Designer

We worked closely as a team rather than treating design, product, and engineering as separate stages of the process.

Research helped us understand the user's problem. Product brought product strategy, requirements, and business context. Engineering brought technical knowledge, constraints, and opportunities. I represented the user and designed the experience while working with the team to find solutions we could all stand behind.

Better decisions happen when the disciplines solving the problem are involved early enough to shape the answer.

Restoration intake begins during a complicated moment.

Someone may be dealing with water, fire, mold, or storm damage while an office employee is simultaneously trying to understand what happened, capture accurate information, determine urgency, and prepare the next team to respond.

Research showed us something important:

The property was the constant.

People weren't.

A property could have different owners, tenants, property managers, callers, onsite contacts, or insurance relationships over its history.

But the job-site address remained the finite location where the work occurred.

That changed the architecture of the experience.

What We Learned

The job-site address was the strongest anchor for the lead.

It could connect:

Property HistoryExisting ContactsPrior ProjectsService AreaDispatchDuplicate Detection

At the same time, research showed that the person calling might not be the owner, customer, or person onsite.

One property could have many people associated with it.

The Problem

A contact-first workflow didn't adequately represent those relationships.

Starting with a person's name could lead the system toward the wrong question:

“Who is this?”

When the more useful first question was:

“Where is the work happening?”

Once we knew the property, we could understand the people associated with it in context.

What We Designed

Start with the address.

Search for the job site before creating another record.

The Workflow

  1. Address

    The user begins typing the job-site address. The field auto-completes possible addresses and returns a verified address for the property.

  2. Property Match

    Does this verified property already exist in XactRestore?

  3. Associated Contacts

    Who has been connected to this property?

  4. Current Relationships

    Who is calling? Who is the customer? Who is onsite?

  5. Loss + Urgency

    What happened and what matters right now?

  6. Assignment + Handoff

    Does the next person have what they need to act?

If a property already existed, the experience could surface its associated contacts and history.

Users could select an existing person, update information, add another relationship, or explicitly resolve a possible duplicate rather than allowing the system to silently merge records.

One research insight changed the architecture of the experience.

We weren't simply reorganizing fields.

We were reorganizing the experience around how the work actually existed in the real world.

The Opportunity

Automate More of the Work Around Intake

Contractors didn't need more administration. They needed the system to do more of the work.

Research pointed toward a larger opportunity: use automation to reduce the administrative work surrounding intake.

The goal wasn't automation for its own sake.

It was to give contractors more time to focus on customers and profitable work while reducing unnecessary administrative effort and the potential for downstream rework.

This represented an opportunity and design direction, not a claimed production result.

The experience could support that direction through:

  • Progressive disclosure

    Ask for information when it becomes relevant.

  • Required-fields-only

    When a caller is frantic, users can show only the fields required to create an actionable lead, streamlining intake and making the process faster.

  • Save Draft

    Allow incomplete information to be finished later without losing what was already known.

  • Duplicate review

    Surface possible matches and give users enough context to make the decision.

  • Handoff summary

    Automate the handoff process so the right information reaches the right team, enabling the project to move forward without unnecessary delays.

Preserve the information the product needs while reducing the work required from the user.

Fail Early: While Change Is Still Cheap

“Fail early” is an Agile principle I carry into the design process. Prototype realistic workflows before we've invested heavily in building them.

The prototype isn't there to prove the design is right.

It's there to help us discover where it's wrong.

Finding an incorrect assumption early gives the team the opportunity to learn, pivot, and test again before the cost of change becomes much greater.

Fail Early→Learn→Pivot→Test Again

Solve It Together

  • Bring Product, Engineering, and Design into the problem while the solution is still open.
  • Give each discipline a voice.
  • Challenge assumptions together.

Shared participation creates shared ownership.

Ship Intentionally

Software is always evolving, but what we deliver should feel intentional and complete, not like we're still working on it.

Every release should feel like the product we intended to build today, even when we already know what comes next.

Think Beyond the Feature

  • Ask what happens upstream, downstream, and six releases from now.
  • What pattern are we establishing?
  • Who will use this information next?
  • Can the solution scale?

A feature can solve today's problem and still create tomorrow's inconsistency.

Complexity Gets More Interesting at Ecosystem Scale

The transitions become part of the product.

The problems I've been solving become even more interesting when complexity expands beyond one application.

When hardware, software, content, AI, services, and community become part of one experience, designing each part well isn't enough.

Hardware×Software×Content×AI×Services×Community

Inspiration→Idea→Create→Make→Share→Inspiration ↻

A person shouldn't have to stop their creative momentum to understand where one product, technology, or capability ends and another begins.

As the ecosystem becomes more powerful, UX has another responsibility:

Keep the power. Hide the complexity.

When that works, people don't experience the technologies underneath the ecosystem.

They experience what they're capable of creating with it.

At the End of the Day

Great UX and business success are not opposing goals.

My responsibility is to advocate for the user.

But user success and business success aren't independent.

They're symbiotic.

A product that fails its users cannot sustain the business.

A solution the business cannot sustain cannot continue creating value for its users.

User Success↔Business Success

That is why I look beyond the interface, to Product, Engineering, technology, effort, cost, strategy, and the business itself.

Senior UX means helping Product, Engineering, Design, and the business find the solution where those needs align.

The job isn't to choose one side.

It's to find the solution that brings them into alignment.

Because that's where sustainable products are built.

Technology can be complicated.
The experience doesn't have to be.
